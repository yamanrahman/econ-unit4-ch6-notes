4.3.6 — 1 Measures of Economic Development ▼

SPEC: 4.3.6 — 1a, 1b, 1c

The Human Development Index (HDI)

Created by Mahbub ul Haq and Amartya Sen for the UNDP (1990). Combines three dimensions into a composite 0–1 score. Reflects Sen’s capabilities approach: development = expanding real freedoms, not just income.

The Three Components — Exactly How Each Is Measured

DimensionIndicatorMinMax
HealthLife expectancy at birth (years)20 yrs85 yrs
EducationMean years of schooling (adults 25+) AND expected years of schooling (children)0 yrs15/18 yrs
Standard of LivingGNI per capita at PPP (2017 US$)$100$75,000
HDI = ∛(Health Index × Education Index × Income Index) — geometric mean of normalised scores; ranges 0 to 1

Classifications: Very High >0.800 (Norway ~0.966) • High 0.700–0.799 (China, Brazil) • Medium 0.550–0.699 (India, Ghana) • Low <0.550 (Niger ~0.394, South Sudan)

Why the geometric mean matters: Unlike a simple average, the geometric mean penalises unbalanced development. A very low health score cannot be compensated by a very high income score. This incentivises governments to invest across all three dimensions rather than optimising for one.

Advantages of HDI

  • Multidimensional: Captures health and education alongside income — aligns with Sen’s capabilities. A person needs health and education to participate fully in economic and social life, not just income.
  • Reveals GDP–development divergence: Cuba and Sri Lanka rank far higher on HDI than their income rank suggests — strong social policy delivers development with modest income. Conversely, Equatorial Guinea has high GDP/capita but low HDI — oil revenues not translated into broad human development.
  • Policy signalling: Decomposing HDI into its three components tells policymakers exactly where development is lagging — health? education? income? — more actionable than a single GDP figure.
  • Consistent and comparable: UNDP calculates HDI annually for 193 countries using standardised methodology — robust cross-country and time-series comparison is possible.
  • Geometric mean avoids substitutability: Countries cannot compensate for very poor health by being very rich — more realistic than arithmetic averaging.

Limitations of HDI

  • Ignores within-country inequality: A high national average HDI can mask extreme poverty among subgroups. The IHDI (Inequality-adjusted HDI) corrects for this but is less widely reported due to data gaps.
  • Ignores political freedom and human rights: Saudi Arabia and UAE score high on HDI but severely restrict political participation and gender equality — dimensions central to Sen’s capabilities approach but absent from HDI.
  • Ignores environmental sustainability: GNI and life expectancy can rise through resource depletion — HDI reflects current welfare not whether it is environmentally sustainable.
  • Arbitrary equal weighting: Why should health, education, and income contribute equally? Prioritisations differ by country and individual circumstance.
  • Data quality: Life expectancy and schooling statistics are unreliable in countries with weak statistical capacity — particularly Sub-Saharan Africa. Published HDI figures may overstate achievement.
  • PPP conversions are imperfect: GNI at PPP is sensitive to the basket of goods chosen and is revised periodically — creates inconsistencies in time-series comparisons.
Best evaluative conclusion: “HDI improves on GDP per capita by incorporating health and education capability, but it still ignores inequality, political freedom, gender equality, and environmental sustainability. A comprehensive picture of development requires a dashboard: HDI + Gini coefficient + Gender Inequality Index + environmental metrics. No single composite can fully capture the multidimensional nature of development.”

4.3.6 — 2a Economic Constraints on Growth and Development ▼

SPEC: 4.3.6 — 2a — all 10 bullet points

1. Volatility of Commodity Prices

Many developing countries depend heavily on primary commodity exports (oil, copper, cocoa, coffee, cotton). Commodity prices are highly volatile — driven by weather, global demand cycles, speculation, and supply shocks.

  • Revenue instability: When prices fall, export earnings collapse → government tax revenue falls → cuts to health, education, and infrastructure spending
  • Investment uncertainty: Firms and governments cannot plan long-run investment when revenues are unpredictable → Harrod-Domar: volatile savings rate means volatile growth rate
  • Dutch Disease: High commodity prices → large foreign exchange inflows → exchange rate appreciates → manufacturing exports become uncompetitive → deindustrialisation. Then when commodity prices fall, no manufacturing base remains.

Example: Nigeria — oil is ~90% of export earnings. The 2014–16 oil price crash halved GDP growth and caused a deep recession. Government was forced to cut social spending just when citizens needed it most.

2. Primary Product Dependency — Prebisch-Singer Hypothesis

Prebisch-Singer Hypothesis (Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer, 1950): The terms of trade (ToT) of primary commodity exporters deteriorate over time relative to manufactured goods prices. Three mechanisms: (1) income elasticity of demand for commodities is low (Engels’ Law) — as incomes rise globally, demand for manufactures grows faster than for commodities; (2) technological progress in primary sectors reduces commodity prices rather than raising wages; (3) manufacturing workers capture productivity gains through union bargaining; agricultural workers cannot. Long-run result: commodity exporters’ import purchasing power falls.
Terms of Trade = (Export Price Index / Import Price Index) × 100 — a fall means each unit of exports buys fewer imports over time

Policy implication — Import Substitution Industrialisation (ISI): Protect infant industries via tariffs/subsidies to diversify out of commodity dependence into manufacturing. Implemented in Latin America 1950s–70s — mixed results.

Evaluation: The 2000s commodity super-cycle (Chinese demand) temporarily reversed the trend — commodity prices rose sharply. But post-2011 prices fell again. The long-run trend broadly holds, but timing and magnitude vary significantly across different commodities.

3. Savings Gap — The Harrod-Domar Model

Harrod-Domar (Roy Harrod 1939, Evsey Domar 1946): Growth rate = savings rate (s) ÷ capital-output ratio (k). Domestic savings fund investment; investment adds capital; capital raises output. If domestic savings are too low to fund required investment, a savings gap (financing gap) exists that must be filled by external sources.
g = s / k — e.g. to achieve 6% growth with k=4, need s=24%. If domestic s=10%, savings gap = 14% of GDP — must be filled by aid, FDI, or borrowing

Why LDCs have low domestic savings: (1) incomes too low to save beyond subsistence; (2) underdeveloped financial systems — banks don’t reach rural populations; (3) high dependency ratios — many dependants per worker reduces household savings capacity.

Policy implication: Foreign aid, FDI, or external borrowing can fill the savings gap — this was the theoretical foundation for large-scale development aid programmes 1950s–70s (Sachs 2005 revived this argument).

Limitation: Assumes fixed capital-output ratio — ignores TFP. Solow (1956): most long-run growth is technology-driven. Easterly (2001): aid-financed investment has not reliably produced growth — absorptive capacity and institutions matter equally.

4. Foreign Currency Gap

Two-gap model (Chenery & Strout, 1966): Even after closing the domestic savings gap via external finance, a country may still lack the foreign currency (hard currency) needed to import capital goods, technology, and intermediate inputs essential for investment. This is a separate binding constraint. The binding constraint switches between the savings gap and the foreign exchange gap depending on the country’s stage of development and export structure.
  • Savings gap: Total investment needed exceeds total domestic savings → filled by ODA, FDI
  • Foreign exchange gap: Import requirements for development exceed export earnings → insufficient hard currency to buy capital goods from abroad

A country can have adequate local-currency savings (e.g. oil revenues) but still lack the diverse export base to earn the specific foreign exchange needed to import technology and machinery. Solutions: export diversification; FDI (brings hard currency directly); foreign borrowing (creates debt risk).

5. Capital Flight

Capital flight: Movement of financial assets out of a country, typically due to political instability, fear of expropriation, high taxes, currency risk, or weak property rights. Reduces domestic savings available for investment, reduces tax revenue, and worsens the foreign exchange gap.
  • Illegal capital flight: False trade invoicing, corruption proceeds, tax evasion through offshore accounts (Switzerland, Cayman Islands, British Virgin Islands)
  • Legal capital flight: Wealthy individuals and firms legally moving assets to countries with stronger property rights, lower taxes, or better returns

Global Financial Integrity: illicit capital flows out of developing countries estimated at $1–1.8 trillion annually — far exceeding total ODA (~$200bn). Sub-Saharan Africa has lost more capital through flight than it has received in aid over 50 years. Creates a vicious circle: weak institutions → capital flight → less tax revenue → weaker public services → weaker institutions.

6. Demographic Factors

Population Size

Large populations can provide scale for domestic markets and a large labour force. But if population grows faster than GDP, GDP per capita falls. Sub-Saharan Africa: population growth ~2.7%/year vs GDP growth ~3–4% → minimal improvement in living standards per head.

Age Distribution and Dependency Ratio

Dependency ratio: Economically dependent (children + elderly) as a proportion of working-age (15–64) population. A high dependency ratio reduces savings rates, increases per-worker costs of education and healthcare, and constrains growth potential.

Demographic dividend: When fertility rates fall, the working-age share temporarily rises — a one-time window for rapid growth. South Korea, Taiwan, Thailand exploited this dividend (1960s–90s) by investing heavily in education for the large working-age cohort and creating manufacturing employment. Sub-Saharan Africa will enter this window as fertility falls — whether it captures the dividend depends on job creation and education investment.

Migration

  • Brain drain (negative): Skilled workers emigrate — doctors, engineers, teachers leave for higher OECD wages, depleting human capital most needed for development. ~65,000 African-trained doctors work in OECD countries.
  • Remittances (positive): Migrants send money home — exceeds FDI and ODA combined in many LDCs. India: $89bn in 2022; Philippines: $36bn. Directly raises household incomes and funds education, health, and housing.
  • Return migration (positive): Migrants return with skills, savings, networks, and entrepreneurial capacity.

7. Debt — Household and Overseas

Overseas (External) Debt

Borrowing from foreign creditors in foreign currency forces governments to divert public spending to debt service. The debt trap: if growth is insufficient to service debt, new borrowing is needed, causing debt to compound. Latin America in the 1980s: IMF structural adjustment programmes required fiscal austerity — cuts to social spending caused the “lost decade.”

Debt overhang: Excessive debt deters private investment — investors expect future tax rises to service debt, reducing expected returns on investment. Even solvent governments suffer lower investment due to overhang.

Household Debt

High-interest informal debt (50–200% per year from informal moneylenders in many developing countries) traps households — unable to accumulate savings or invest in human capital. Reduces consumption and enterprise investment capacity.

8. Access to Credit and Banking

Without formal banking and credit access, individuals and small firms cannot invest beyond current income, smooth consumption across shocks, or accumulate business capital. The unbanked (estimated 1.4 billion globally, World Bank Findex) rely on informal lenders or forgo investment entirely.

Financial exclusion compounds poverty: the poor pay higher rates for informal credit; cannot save securely; cannot access insurance against health or weather shocks. Mobile banking has dramatically expanded access in East Africa (M-Pesa: 50 million users in Kenya, Tanzania, and beyond) — leapfrogging traditional bank branch infrastructure. Microfinance (see strategies section) is the main institutional response to financial exclusion.

9. Infrastructure

Inadequate physical infrastructure — roads, ports, electricity, water, telecoms — raises transaction costs, reduces market integration, and constrains all other investment. Infrastructure is a complementary input: other investments (factories, farms, schools) are less productive without it.

  • Transport: Poor roads prevent rural farmers from reaching markets — forced to sell to local intermediaries at low prices. World Bank: improving rural road connectivity can raise agricultural incomes 30–50%.
  • Power: Unreliable electricity forces firms to invest in private generators — raises costs, reduces competitiveness. Sub-Saharan Africa: power outages reduce GDP growth by ~1–2% per year.
  • Ports/logistics: Inefficient ports and customs raise import/export costs — equivalent to additional tariffs. Landlocked countries face especially high logistics costs.

World Bank: 1% increase in infrastructure investment raises long-run GDP by approximately 1.5% in developing countries. Infrastructure is a classic market failure (public good / natural monopoly) requiring government provision or strong regulation of private providers.

10. Education and Skills

Low levels of education and vocational skills reduce labour productivity, constrain technology adoption, and limit the industries that can develop. Education creates a poverty trap: poor families cannot afford quality education → low human capital → low wages → cannot fund children’s education.

Quality vs quantity: Many LDCs have expanded enrolment without improving learning outcomes — the binding constraint is often learning quality, not access. PISA-equivalent assessments show very low foundational literacy and numeracy even in countries with near-universal primary enrolment (e.g. India, Pakistan). Girls’ education has the highest social return — reduces fertility, improves child health, and increases female labour force participation.

4.3.6 — 2b Non-Economic Constraints on Growth and Development ▼

SPEC: 4.3.6 — 2b — corruption, poor governance, civil wars, migration, terrorism

Corruption

Corruption: The abuse of entrusted power for private gain — bribery, embezzlement, nepotism, and manipulation of contracts and regulations. Raises transaction costs, misallocates resources, and deters investment.
  • Public funds embezzled → roads, schools, hospitals not built; medicines not purchased
  • Firms pay “speed money” to operate — functions as a private tax on enterprise, reducing profitability and investment incentives
  • Contracts awarded to connected firms rather than most efficient → lower-quality public works at higher cost
  • FDI deterred — investors prefer transparent, stable jurisdictions with predictable costs

Transparency International CPI: most corrupt countries (Somalia, South Sudan, Syria) are consistently among the world’s poorest. Causality is bidirectional — poverty generates corruption by creating incentives for officials to supplement low salaries — making it extremely difficult to escape without simultaneous governance reform and economic development.

Poor Governance

Good governance — accountable, transparent, and effective government — is foundational to development. Poor governance encompasses: weak rule of law (contracts not enforced, property rights insecure), unaccountable political systems (no electoral check on behaviour), ineffective public administration (policies not implemented), and regulatory failure (markets poorly supervised).

Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson (2001): institutions — the formal and informal rules of society — are the primary long-run determinant of development outcomes. Extractive institutions (concentrated power, insecure property rights, elite capture) persistently produce underdevelopment. Inclusive institutions (broad property rights, rule of law, political pluralism) sustain growth. North vs South Korea: starting from identical institutions in 1945, divergent institutions since the 38th parallel split produced radically different development outcomes.

Civil Wars

Civil conflict is one of the most devastating constraints on development. Paul Collier (The Bottom Billion, 2007): civil war is one of the four key “development traps.”

  • Destruction of capital: Infrastructure, factories, hospitals, and schools physically destroyed — reversing decades of investment
  • Human displacement: Refugees and IDPs lose livelihoods; children’s schooling disrupted — permanent human capital loss
  • FDI deterrence: Investment requires security — civil war makes long-run investment impossible
  • Government spending diverted: Military spending crowds out health, education, and infrastructure
  • Conflict trap: Countries emerging from civil war have a 44% probability of returning to conflict within 5 years — poverty → grievances → conflict → more poverty. Breaking the cycle requires rapid job creation and justice/reconciliation, not just economic policy.

Sahel example: Terrorist insurgencies in Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger (Al-Qaeda and Islamic State affiliates) since 2012 have reversed development gains — school closures, farmer displacement, and aid worker withdrawal have pushed millions into acute food insecurity.

Migration (Non-Economic Aspects)

Migration’s economic effects (remittances vs brain drain) are covered under economic constraints. The non-economic development consequences include:

  • Social disruption: Large-scale emigration of young workers disrupts community structures and leaves elderly populations without care — reducing social capital in origin countries
  • Governance brain drain: When educated professionals (civil servants, lawyers, accountants) emigrate, institutional capacity is depleted — government services deteriorate even if GDP is stable
  • Forced migration (refugees): Conflict-driven displacement disrupts children’s education, destroys social networks, and causes lasting psychological trauma — with long-run impacts on human capital and social cohesion in origin and host countries alike

Terrorism

Terrorism constrains development through multiple channels beyond direct casualties:

  • Tourism collapse: Tourist arrivals fall sharply after attacks. Tunisia: HDI improvement stalled after the 2015 Sousse and Bardo attacks — tourism revenue fell >25% in one year. Egypt’s tourism industry similarly devastated after the 2015 Sinai plane bombing.
  • FDI deterrence: Political risk scores decline → multinationals relocate to more stable jurisdictions
  • Security spending: Government resources diverted from development investment to counter-terrorism and military — crowding out social spending
  • Trade disruption: Terrorism in key corridors raises insurance and security costs for exporters — equivalent to a trade cost increase
  • Human capital loss: Not just direct casualties — fear reduces labour mobility, education attendance, and economic participation broadly
Non-economic factors are consistently underweighted in exam answers. The spec lists all five explicitly. For each, explain the mechanism through which it constrains development — don’t just name it. The key evaluative insight: “Non-economic constraints are often more fundamental than economic ones because they determine whether economic policy can be effective at all. Capital can be transferred via aid; good institutions cannot.” Cite Acemoglu for governance and Collier for conflict.
4.3.6 — 3a Market-Orientated Strategies ▼

SPEC: 4.3.6 — 3a — trade liberalisation, FDI, removal of subsidies, privatisation, floating exchange rates, microfinance

Market-orientated strategies rely on price signals and private initiative rather than government direction. Associated with the Washington Consensus (Williamson, 1989) — 10 market-liberal reforms promoted by the IMF, World Bank, and US Treasury from the 1980s.

Trade Liberalisation

Reducing or eliminating tariffs, quotas, and non-tariff barriers. Theoretical basis: Ricardo’s comparative advantage — specialise in relatively lower-cost goods and trade for others → mutual gains.

Benefits
  • Access to global markets enables scale economies
  • Import competition forces domestic efficiency
  • Technology transfer via imported capital goods
  • Lower import prices for consumers and producers
  • Export-led growth: South Korea, Taiwan, China exploited open markets
Limitations
  • Prebisch-Singer: comparative advantage in commodities → deteriorating ToT
  • Infant industry problem: nascent industries destroyed before developing
  • Asymmetric: OECD agricultural subsidies (CAP, US farm bill) create unfair competition
  • Premature capital account liberalisation → Asia 1997 crisis

Promotion of FDI

Attracting Foreign Direct Investment via Export Processing Zones (EPZs), tax holidays, bilateral investment treaties, and repatriation guarantees.

Benefits
  • Fills Harrod-Domar savings gap with foreign capital
  • Technology and management spillovers to domestic firms
  • Employment creation and wage income
  • Integration into global value chains (GVCs)
  • Reduces foreign exchange gap directly
Limitations
  • Transfer pricing: profits shifted abroad → limited tax revenue (OECD BEPS)
  • Enclave FDI: resource extraction, minimal local linkages
  • Dutch Disease: commodity FDI appreciates exchange rate
  • Technology spillovers require absorptive capacity — limited without human capital

Removal of Government Subsidies

Rationale: subsidies distort price signals, protect inefficient producers, and misallocate resources. Removing them forces efficiency and frees fiscal resources for more productive investment.

Efficiency case
  • Removes distortions — price signals direct resources to highest-value uses
  • Frees government budget for infrastructure and education
  • Eliminates rent-seeking by subsidy recipients
  • Improves public finances — reduces deficit/debt
Risks
  • Food and energy subsidies protect the poor — removal causes immediate hardship
  • Sudden removal can cause social unrest (Egypt bread subsidies, India fuel subsidies)
  • Agricultural subsidies support food security — removal exposes farmers to price volatility
  • Infant industries may legitimately need temporary subsidy support

Privatisation

Transferring state-owned enterprises (SOEs) to private ownership. Rationale: private firms face profit motive, competition, and capital market discipline — more efficient than state enterprises insulated from market pressure.

Potential benefits
  • Profit motive reduces waste and overstaffing
  • Reduces fiscal burden of loss-making SOEs
  • Generates one-off revenue (privatisation proceeds)
  • Private management expertise and investment
Limitations in LDC context
  • Without competition, private monopoly replaces public monopoly
  • Natural monopolies (water, electricity, rail): profit motive conflicts with universal service
  • Weak regulatory capacity in LDCs — privatised firms are poorly supervised
  • Social objectives (rural access, affordability) may be abandoned for profit

Floating Exchange Rate Systems

Exchange rate determined by supply and demand in currency markets without central bank intervention. Rationale: automatic adjustment to external shocks restores competitiveness without policy action.

Benefits
  • Automatic adjustment: commodity price fall → depreciation → exports cheaper → competitiveness restored
  • Independent monetary policy retained (Mundell’s trilemma)
  • No need for large foreign exchange reserves to defend a peg
  • Avoids currency crises from defending an overvalued fixed rate
Limitations for LDCs
  • Volatility deters trade and investment — creates uncertainty for exporters/importers
  • Depreciation raises import prices → inflation (especially for food and fuel importers)
  • Speculative capital flows cause excessive volatility in thin LDC currency markets
  • Commodity exporters: large swings in domestic revenue as commodity prices change

Microfinance Schemes

Microfinance: Small loans, savings accounts, and insurance for individuals excluded from formal banking — particularly the rural poor without collateral. Pioneered by Muhammad Yunus and the Grameen Bank, Bangladesh (1983). Group lending model: small groups (typically 5 women) are collectively responsible — social pressure substitutes for collateral. Average loan ~$200; repayment rates historically 97%+.
Potential benefits
  • Expands credit access for those excluded from formal banking
  • Enables micro-enterprise investment — small businesses generating income
  • Women-focused: improves female economic agency and empowerment
  • Reduces dependence on informal moneylenders (50–200% interest)
  • Mobile microfinance (M-Shwari, Kenya) dramatically reduces delivery costs
Limitations
  • Banerjee & Duflo RCT evidence: microfinance has not consistently lifted borrowers out of poverty
  • Interest rates still 20–40% in many schemes — limits net investment return
  • Over-indebtedness: competing MFIs → multiple loans → debt spiral
  • Group pressure causes social harm when members cannot repay
  • Micro-enterprises face demand ceiling — credit alone cannot overcome infrastructure and market access barriers
Essential microfinance evaluation: “Banerjee and Duflo (2011, Poor Economics) conducted RCTs in multiple countries and found that while microfinance expanded credit access, it did not consistently increase household incomes or lift borrowers out of poverty. The most significant impact was reducing reliance on very high-cost informal credit. Microfinance is a useful poverty-alleviation tool, but not a development strategy on its own.”
4.3.6 — 3b Interventionist Development Strategies ▼

SPEC: 4.3.6 — 3b — human capital, protectionism, managed exchange rates, infrastructure, joint ventures, buffer stocks

Development of Human Capital

Government investment in education and healthcare is the foundational supply-side intervention. Human capital theory (Mincer 1958, Becker 1964): each year of education raises worker productivity and earnings by ~8–10% (UNICEF). Investment in girls’ education has the highest social return: reduces fertility, improves child health outcomes, and increases female labour force participation. South Korea expanded tertiary education in the 1960s–80s to create the skilled workforce that powered electronics and automotive export industries.

Protectionism — Infant Industry Argument

Infant industry argument: New domestic industries cannot compete with established foreign firms that benefit from economies of scale, learning-by-doing, and existing supply chains. Temporary protection (tariffs, subsidies, import quotas) allows infant industries time to develop scale and efficiency until they become internationally competitive. Associated with Hamilton (1791), List (1841), and the East Asian developmental state.

South Korean model: Government selected strategic industries (POSCO steel, Hyundai shipbuilding, Samsung electronics) and provided: protected domestic markets via tariffs; directed credit at below-market rates; export subsidies; public R&D. Crucially, protection was conditional on export performance — firms that failed to become competitive lost support. South Korea went from $80 per capita income in 1960 to OECD membership by 2000.

Case for infant industry protection
  • Allows firms to develop scale economies before global competition
  • Learning-by-doing: productivity improves with cumulative experience
  • Overcomes coordination failures: complementary industries must develop together
  • Chang (2002): all current developed nations industrialised behind tariff walls — “kicking away the ladder”
  • Dynamic comparative advantage: protection creates future competitive sectors
Limitations
  • Government failure: difficult to correctly identify which industries to protect — information problem
  • Rent-seeking: protected industries lobby to maintain protection permanently
  • Latin American ISI: many industries remained uncompetitive for decades, contributing to 1980s debt crises
  • Retaliation risk: trading partners may impose retaliatory tariffs
  • Consumer welfare loss: higher prices for protected goods

Managed Exchange Rates

Government or central bank intervenes in currency markets to maintain a target rate or limit volatility. Types: fixed pegs, managed floats, currency boards. Development rationale: an undervalued exchange rate acts as an implicit subsidy to all export industries — makes exports cheaper on world markets, supporting export-led growth. China maintained an undervalued Renminbi through the 1990s–2000s, supporting manufacturing export competitiveness.

Benefits
  • Reduces exchange rate volatility → lowers uncertainty for exporters and importers
  • Undervalued rate boosts export competitiveness — supports industrialisation
  • Signals price stability — anchors inflation expectations
  • Prevents speculative attack in thin LDC currency markets
Risks
  • Requires large foreign exchange reserves to defend the rate
  • Overvalued rate: exports uncompetitive → BoP deteriorates → forced sharp devaluation
  • Sacrifices independent monetary policy (Mundell’s trilemma)
  • “Currency manipulation” label from trading partners → retaliatory tariffs

Infrastructure Development

Government investment in roads, railways, ports, power generation, water supply, and telecoms. Addresses market failures: natural monopoly (private firms under-provide), positive externalities (infrastructure benefits all users), and public good characteristics. Reduces transaction costs and enables all other investment to be more productive.

Ethiopia’s government-led infrastructure investment (roads, rail, telecom, hydro dams) in the 2000s–2010s contributed to consistent 8–10% GDP growth — fastest in Sub-Saharan Africa. China’s Belt and Road Initiative exports this model — financing infrastructure in developing countries as part of a geopolitical and commercial strategy. World Bank: 1% increase in infrastructure investment → ~1.5% long-run GDP gain in LDCs.

Promoting Joint Ventures with TNCs

Joint venture with a TNC: A partnership between a domestic firm (or government) and a transnational corporation, where both parties contribute capital and expertise and share profits and risks. Government mandates or incentivises JVs as a condition of market access — ensuring technology transfer and skills development that would not occur with wholly foreign-owned subsidiaries.

China’s JV model (1980s–2000s): Foreign car manufacturers (VW, GM, Toyota) were required to form 50:50 JVs with Chinese state-owned firms to access the Chinese market. This forced technology transfer to Chinese partners. Chinese firms (BYD, Geely) have since developed the capacity to produce competitive vehicles independently.

Benefits
  • Forces technology and management skill transfer to domestic partner
  • Retains partial domestic ownership — profits not fully repatriated
  • Builds local supply chain linkages
  • Domestic partner gains access to TNC’s global distribution networks
Limitations
  • TNCs may ring-fence core technology — transfer only older processes
  • Mandating JVs may deter FDI if TNCs prefer wholly owned subsidiaries
  • Domestic partner may lack absorptive capacity
  • WTO disputes: US–China trade war partly driven by forced technology transfer allegations

Buffer Stock Schemes

Buffer stock scheme: A government or international body buys and stores a commodity when its price falls below a target floor price, and sells from stores when the price rises above a target ceiling price. Stabilises commodity prices within a band, protecting producer incomes in LDCs from price volatility.

Mechanism: Price falls below floor → buffer stock manager buys → demand increases → price supported. Price rises above ceiling → buffer stock manager sells → supply increases → price restrained. Price stabilised within floor–ceiling band.

Potential benefits
  • Stabilises commodity export revenues — reduces income volatility for LDC producers
  • Allows producers to plan investment with more predictable incomes
  • Food buffer stocks protect consumers from price spikes — improves food security
  • Prevents race-to-the-bottom among competing commodity exporters
Problems
  • Storage costs: physical commodities deteriorate — especially perishable food
  • If floor is above long-run equilibrium, stocks accumulate indefinitely — International Tin Agreement collapsed 1985; International Coffee Agreement 1989
  • Structural vs cyclical falls: only sustainable for temporary price swings, not long-run ToT deterioration (Prebisch-Singer)
  • Coordination: non-participating producers free-ride — sell at supported prices while buffer manager buys
  • Large financial reserves required to fund purchases when prices fall
Buffer stock key evaluation: “Buffer stock schemes can only function if price fluctuations are cyclical rather than structural. If the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis is correct and commodity prices are on a long-run declining trend, buffer stocks will accumulate indefinitely, eventually exhausting financial reserves and collapsing. This is exactly what happened to virtually all major international commodity agreements in the 20th century. They are at best a temporary stabilisation tool, not a development strategy.”
4.3.6 — 3c Other Development Strategies ▼

SPEC: 4.3.6 — 3c — Lewis model, tourism, primary industries, debt relief, aid

Industrialisation — The Lewis Dual-Sector Model

W. Arthur Lewis (1954): Developing economies have two sectors: (1) Traditional agricultural sector — surplus labour (workers with MLP ≈ 0, paid at subsistence wage). Labour can be removed from agriculture with minimal loss of agricultural output. (2) Modern industrial sector — attracts surplus agricultural labour at a wage slightly above subsistence. Industrial profits are reinvested → sector expands → absorbs more labour → structural transformation from agriculture to industry = development.

Lewis Turning Point: When all surplus agricultural labour is absorbed, further labour demand requires real wage increases. At this point wages rise economy-wide, the labour share of income increases, and cheap-labour-driven industrialisation ends. China reached this turning point approximately 2010–2015 — explaining rapidly rising Chinese wages and the strategic shift toward higher-value, less labour-intensive production.

Strengths
  • Describes East Asian industrialisation well — Japan, South Korea, China all followed a Lewis-type structural transformation path
  • Identifies reinvestment mechanism — connects capital accumulation, structural change, and development in one framework
  • Provides policy rationale for attracting industrial investment via EPZs, infrastructure, and FDI incentives
Limitations
  • Agricultural MLP ≈ 0 assumption: Green Revolution raised agricultural productivity — surplus labour assumption weaker than Lewis thought
  • Harris-Todaro (1970): urban unemployment trap — if industrial job creation lags migration, urban slums grow rather than industrial employment
  • Profit repatriation: MNC-dominated industrial sector → profits leave country → Lewis reinvestment mechanism breaks down
  • Assumes passive private sector — East Asian development actually required active state direction, not just capital availability

Development of Tourism

Tourism is a major service export — tourists spend foreign currency domestically, providing foreign exchange earnings equivalent to goods exports. Key development potential: low capital requirements relative to manufacturing; uses natural and cultural assets already present; creates employment across skill levels.

Benefits
  • Foreign exchange earnings — reduces the foreign currency gap
  • Employment creation in hospitality, transport, retail, services
  • Multiplier effect: tourist spending circulates, supporting further employment
  • Infrastructure development benefits local populations too
  • Diversifies away from commodity dependence using natural/cultural assets
Limitations
  • Leakage: foreign-owned hotels and imported goods → revenue leaves country
  • Seasonality: revenue concentrated in peak seasons → worker income volatility
  • Environmental damage: over-tourism degrades the natural assets that attract visitors
  • Vulnerability: terrorism, pandemics (COVID-19), and political instability collapse arrivals instantly — Thailand lost 83% of tourists in 2020
  • Low wages: tourism employment often low-skilled and low-paid — limited human capital development

Development of Primary Industries

Rather than immediately industrialising, some countries develop their natural resource base — mining, agriculture, forestry, fishing — as a foundation for growth. Primary industry provides: export revenues, government tax income, employment, and foreign exchange to fund future diversification.

Case for primary industry development
  • Exploits existing comparative advantage — uses what the country already has
  • Botswana model: diamond revenues + strong institutions + Debswana (50/50 JV with De Beers) → upper-middle-income country within 50 years of independence
  • Agricultural development reduces poverty directly: 60–70% of the world’s extreme poor are rural farmers — raising their productivity is the most direct poverty-reduction route
  • Green Revolution: improved seeds, irrigation, fertilisers → dramatically reduced famine in South Asia, Southeast Asia
Risks
  • Resource curse: oil/mineral wealth associated with conflict, corruption, weak institutions (Nigeria, DRC, Angola) — Collier (2007)
  • Dutch Disease: resource export revenues appreciate exchange rate → other tradeable sectors uncompetitive
  • Prebisch-Singer: commodity ToT deteriorate long-run — revenues fall relative to import costs over time
  • Price volatility creates macroeconomic instability — unreliable development base

Debt Relief

Debt relief: Reduction, rescheduling, or cancellation of developing country external debt. Frees government spending from debt service toward development investment. Key initiatives: HIPC Initiative (1996) — IMF and World Bank debt cancellation for qualifying heavily indebted poor countries; MDRI (2006) — full cancellation of debt owed to IMF, World Bank, and African Development Bank for HIPC graduates.

Impact: 37 countries have received HIPC relief, saving approximately $100bn in debt service. Post-HIPC countries showed increases in health and education spending as fiscal space opened. Child mortality rates fell and school enrolment increased in many beneficiaries. Limitation: Some countries re-accumulated unsustainable debt through new commercial and Chinese lending after receiving HIPC relief — problem not permanently solved. Moral hazard: expectation of future relief may encourage over-borrowing.

Aid (ODA)

Official Development Assistance (ODA): Concessional financial flows (grants or low-interest loans) from developed country governments to developing countries — bilateral (government-to-government) or multilateral (via World Bank, UNDP, regional development banks). UN target: 0.7% of donor GNI. Only Scandinavia, Luxembourg, and Netherlands consistently meet this. Types: bilateral (often “tied” — must buy donor country goods), multilateral (more needs-based), humanitarian (emergency relief), technical assistance.
Case for aid — Sachs (2005)
  • Fills Harrod-Domar savings/financing gap
  • Targeted interventions have strong RCT evidence: bed nets, oral rehydration therapy, vaccines (Banerjee & Duflo)
  • PEPFAR: measurable reduction in HIV/AIDS mortality in Sub-Saharan Africa
  • Can break poverty traps by funding multiple complementary investments simultaneously
  • HIPC debt relief: measurable improvements in social spending outcomes
Case against aid — Easterly, Moyo
  • Easterly (2006): $2.3tn in aid since 1960 — many recipients remain poor
  • Moyo (2009): aid funds bad governance — governments accountable to donors, not citizens
  • Dependency: reduces incentives for domestic revenue mobilisation
  • Dutch Disease: large inflows appreciate exchange rate → export competitiveness harmed
  • Fungibility: aid frees government resources for military or elite spending
  • Tied aid: may reduce real value by 15–30%
4.3.6 — 3d International Institutions — World Bank, IMF & NGOs ▼

SPEC: 4.3.6 — 3d — World Bank, IMF, NGOs

The World Bank

World Bank: International financial institution providing long-term loans, grants, and technical assistance to developing countries. Founded 1944 (Bretton Woods). Two main bodies: IBRD (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development — loans to middle-income countries at near-market rates) and IDA (International Development Association — concessional 0% interest loans and grants to the poorest 74 countries, 25–40 year repayment periods).

What the World Bank Does

  • Project finance: Long-term loans for infrastructure, agriculture, health, education, institutional reform — IDA is the largest single source of development finance for low-income countries
  • Development policy lending: Budget support conditional on specific policy reforms — governance improvements, social protection expansion, fiscal reform
  • Knowledge and data: World Development Report, World Development Indicators — most comprehensive global development dataset and annual policy analysis
  • Debt relief: Participates in HIPC and MDRI debt cancellation programmes
  • Technical assistance: Builds institutional capacity in tax policy, financial regulation, public financial management
Benefits
  • IDA provides concessional finance unavailable from private markets for poorest countries
  • HIPC/MDRI debt relief freed ~$100bn in debt service
  • Produces critical development data and research
  • Technical assistance builds institutional capacity
  • Increasingly uses RCT evidence to design effective interventions
Criticisms
  • Structural adjustment conditionality imposed market-fundamentalist reforms harming development (Stiglitz 2002)
  • Governance: voting shares weighted by financial contribution — US has effective veto; rich country priorities dominate
  • Some infrastructure projects have caused negative social/environmental impacts (dam displacement, deforestation)
  • Bureaucratic — slow disbursement, complex requirements disadvantage low-capacity governments

The International Monetary Fund (IMF)

IMF: International organisation of 190 members providing short-term balance of payments support to prevent currency crises from destabilising the international financial system. Founded 1944 (Bretton Woods). Loans are conditional on macroeconomic adjustment: fiscal consolidation, monetary tightening, exchange rate adjustment, and structural reforms.

IMF Functions

  • Emergency BoP lending: Standby Arrangements and Extended Fund Facilities for countries facing currency crises — conditional on stabilisation programmes
  • Concessional lending for LDCs: Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust (PRGT) — zero-interest loans to low-income countries with protracted BoP problems
  • Surveillance: Annual Article IV consultations — reviews each member’s economic policies; IMF assessments influence bond market perceptions and investor confidence
  • SDRs (Special Drawing Rights): International reserve asset — allocated to members to supplement official reserves during global liquidity crises. COVID-19: $650bn SDR allocation in August 2021 provided immediate liquidity support to all members.
  • Technical assistance: Fiscal, monetary, and financial sector policy capacity-building
Benefits
  • Lender of last resort: prevents currency crises from becoming full economic collapses
  • Conditionality enforces macroeconomic discipline that prevents fiscal profligacy
  • PRGT concessional lending for LDCs — provides BoP support unavailable elsewhere
  • SDR allocations: emergency liquidity without conditionality during global crises
  • Post-2008 reforms: more flexibility, acknowledged fiscal multiplier underestimation
Criticisms
  • Stiglitz (2002): conditionality imposed “market fundamentalism” — austerity deepened recessions in East Asia 1997 and Argentina 2001
  • Procyclical: demanded fiscal cuts during downturns — opposite of Keynesian prescription
  • Social impact: cuts to health/education subsidies hit poorest hardest
  • Governance: quota-based voting — US effective veto; agenda reflects rich country interests
  • “IMF stigma”: countries delay approaching IMF for fear of signalling weakness to financial markets

Key distinction to know: World Bank = long-term development finance (roads, schools, governance). IMF = short-term BoP crisis management (emergency lending to stabilise currencies). Both headquartered in Washington DC and both subject to rich-country governance dominance — the main institutional critique.

Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs)

NGO: A non-profit, voluntary organisation independent of government that works on development, humanitarian, or advocacy goals. Ranges from community organisations to global institutions (Oxfam, CARE, MSF, BRAC, GiveDirectly). In 2022, NGOs channelled approximately 15–20% of total ODA.

Role of NGOs in Development

  • Service delivery: Provide health, education, and social protection where government services are absent — MSF operates in conflict zones where governments cannot function
  • Humanitarian response: First responders in crises — Oxfam, WFP, UNHCR coordinate emergency food, shelter, and healthcare
  • Advocacy: Campaign for debt relief, fair trade, climate finance — Jubilee 2000 debt relief campaign was instrumental in creating the HIPC Initiative
  • Microfinance and enterprise: Many NGOs operate microfinance and market linkage programmes for rural producers
  • Research and evidence: GiveDirectly’s unconditional cash transfer RCTs; Oxfam’s inequality research; BRAC’s ultra-poor graduation model evidence
Advantages
  • More flexible and innovative than government programmes — can pilot new approaches rapidly
  • Often more accountable to beneficiaries than remote aid agencies
  • Operate in difficult environments (conflict zones, remote areas) where governments cannot
  • BRAC (Bangladesh): world’s largest NGO — 100+ million beneficiaries in health, microfinance, education
  • GiveDirectly RCT evidence: unconditional cash transfers have robust positive development impact
Limitations
  • Not democratically elected or accountable to citizens — operate on donor priorities, not local needs
  • May undermine state capacity: parallel NGO services reduce government incentive to build its own systems
  • Fragmented: thousands of NGOs in same country create coordination failures and duplication of effort
  • Transaction costs: compliance with multiple donor requirements consumes scarce organisational resources
  • May impose Western cultural assumptions — external priorities on local communities without genuine participation
Examiner’s key distinction: the World Bank and IMF are often confused. World Bank = long-term development (20–40 year project loans); IMF = short-term BoP stabilisation (typically 3–5 year programme loans). For both: the primary criticism is that they are governed by rich countries (US effective veto), historically imposed pro-market conditionality without regard for social impacts, and have been slow to reform governance to give developing countries more voice. Cite Stiglitz (2002) Globalisation and its Discontents for the sharpest critique.

4 marksExplain why GDP per capita may be an imperfect measure of economic development.

Model Answer

GDP per capita measures average income but fails to capture several key dimensions of development. First, it ignores the distribution of income: a high average masks extreme inequality where the poor see no gain despite rising national income. Second, it omits non-income dimensions of welfare — health outcomes, educational attainment, and political freedom — which Amartya Sen's capabilities approach identifies as central to human development.

Third, the informal economy is excluded, understating real living standards in many developing countries where subsistence agriculture and informal services are significant. Fourth, GDP can rise through environmental degradation — depletion of natural capital that reduces future productive potential. Finally, for countries with large foreign ownership of productive assets, GDP overstates residents' income — GNI per capita is more appropriate.

Mark scheme: 1 mark per well-developed point up to 4. Look for: distribution/inequality; non-income dimensions/Sen; informal economy; GNI vs GDP; environmental degradation.
8 marksUsing the Harrod-Domar model, explain the role of savings and investment in economic development.

Model Answer

The Harrod-Domar model (Roy Harrod 1939, Evsey Domar 1946) states that the growth rate of GDP equals the savings rate divided by the capital-output ratio: g = s/k. The capital-output ratio (k) measures the units of capital required to produce one unit of GDP — reflecting the economy's technology.

Savings are crucial because they finance investment: households and firms save a proportion of income (s), which is channelled through the financial system into capital investment. Investment adds to the capital stock, expanding productive capacity. With k fixed, more savings → more investment → more capital → more output → higher growth rate.

For developing countries, this identifies a "financing gap": domestic savings rates are too low to fund the investment needed for rapid development. If a country requires a 5% growth rate and k = 4, it needs s = 20%. If domestic savings only provide 10%, there is a 10% of GDP financing gap that must be filled by foreign aid, FDI, or external borrowing. This was the theoretical justification for large-scale development aid in the 1950s–70s.

Evaluation (for Band 4): The model is limited by its assumption of a fixed capital-output ratio — it ignores total factor productivity and the role of technology. Solow (1956) showed that most long-run growth comes from TFP, not capital accumulation. Easterly (2001) found that aid-financed investment has not reliably translated into higher growth, suggesting that absorptive capacity, institutional quality, and human capital are equally important constraints.

Band 4: Accurate formula and explanation. Chain of causation from savings → investment → capital → growth. Application to developing country financing gap. Evaluation: limitations of fixed k; TFP; Easterly's empirical challenge.
20 marksEvaluate the view that foreign aid is the most effective way to promote economic development in low-income countries.

Band 4 Model Plan

Define: ODA — concessional financial flows from developed to developing country governments. Development — broader than growth: HDI, poverty reduction, Sen's capabilities. Note: question asks about effectiveness relative to alternatives.

For (Sachs): Harrod-Domar financing gap — LDCs lack domestic savings. Aid fills this gap, funding investment in health, education, infrastructure. Targeted interventions (PEPFAR, bed nets, ORS) have strong RCT evidence (Banerjee & Duflo). HIPC debt relief freed public spending for social investment — measurable improvements in child mortality and school enrolment in recipient countries.

Against (Easterly, Moyo): Despite $2.3 trillion in aid since 1960, many recipients remain poor. Moyo (2009): aid perpetuates bad governance by funding governments that are accountable to donors rather than citizens. Dependency: reduces domestic revenue mobilisation. Dutch Disease: large inflows appreciate exchange rate, undermining export competitiveness. Fungibility: aid frees resources for military spending or elite consumption.

Alternative 1 — Trade: Trade access for LDC exports to OECD markets would deliver income gains potentially exceeding ODA. The EU's Everything But Arms (EBA) initiative provides duty-free access for least developed countries — sustainable income gain without dependency. But: structural constraints mean many LDCs lack capacity to exploit trade opportunities.

Alternative 2 — Institutions: Acemoglu (2001): institutional quality is the foundational determinant of development. Aid without institutional reform may be wasted or captured by elites. South Korea and Botswana developed rapidly with relatively little aid — strong institutional foundations were more important than external finance.

Alternative 3 — Domestic revenue mobilisation: IMF: LDC tax revenue as % of GDP averages ~15% vs 35–45% in OECD. Broadening the tax base (reducing informality, improving enforcement, combating BEPS) is more sustainable than aid and avoids dependency and governance distortions.

Evaluation: The dichotomy is false — aid works under specific conditions. Banerjee and Duflo: targeted interventions with RCT evidence (health, primary education) reliably improve outcomes; general budget support has weak evidence. Institutional context determines whether aid produces development or dependency.

Conclusion: Aid is not the most effective lever on its own. Trade access, institutional reform, and domestic revenue mobilisation are more fundamental for sustainable development. Aid is most valuable as a catalyst for high-social-return interventions that markets underprovide — conditional on reasonable institutional quality to ensure resources reach intended beneficiaries.

Band 4 criteria: Multiple named economists (Sachs, Easterly, Moyo, Banerjee & Duflo, Acemoglu). Evidence-based evaluation weighing different types of aid. Genuine alternatives considered. Qualified conclusion specifying conditions under which aid is effective.

Explain
